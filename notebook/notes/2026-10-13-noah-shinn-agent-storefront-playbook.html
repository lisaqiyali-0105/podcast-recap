8 lessons Noah Shinn taught me about the agent storefront

Noah Shinn – Building Instinct: The Personal Agent →

01On Business Model

Free for the user, the merchant pays for distribution

“The user is getting a free, great experience and the merchants on the other end who are benefiting from the business are paying to be a part of that platform. So I see a blanket transaction take rate being enforced across the platform, which is just us exchanging distribution for being able to serve products on behalf of merchants.”

“we still don’t know where along this curve of distribution power versus take rate that we’re going to be.”

The PM Translation

DD Workspace already sits where deal teams decide what to buy: expert calls, third-party data, broker research, outside advisors. If the agent books and buys on the user’s behalf, AlphaSense becomes a sales channel for those providers — and can earn from them, not only from seats.

The Investor Read

Noah’s reference points: payments players split only ~2–2.5% (he isn’t chasing that slice), Shopify ~2.5–3%, Amazon 10%+, Apple 30% on in-app purchases. Where a company lands on that curve depends on how much buying it controls, not on how good the product is. The diligence question: what share of the user’s purchases actually go through the agent? Instinct claims over $1B a year in transactions, about half of it travel.

In Practice

List the three things a DDW user pays a third party for during a deal, and estimate the spend per deal. That’s the transaction volume a take rate would apply to.

02On Distribution

Be the incumbents’ channel, not their competitor

“I’m not saying that we’re in the business of trying to disrupt those businesses. I think they do provide quite a bit of value from the data that they’ve collected over time and the networks that they have.”

“This is how users on instinct are interacting with your business now already. What can we do in collaboration to make that a better experience for both sides where it makes sense for your business and makes sense for our business?”

The PM Translation

This is the leverage you flagged — and it can also run against AlphaSense. Once deal teams work through one agent, AlphaSense content could end up as a supplier inside someone else’s storefront: Claude, ChatGPT, or Rogo calling AlphaSense. Decide which side you’re on for each workflow before the market decides it for you.

The Investor Read

His partner playbook avoids a fight with incumbents: show the incumbent how its users already use it through Instinct, turn it on for ~1% of users and A/B test, then scale only if it helps both sides. A founder who can close deals this way can grow without being cut off by the platforms.

In Practice

For one data partner, draft the one-pager Noah describes: “here’s how your users already reach you through DDW.”

03On Exposure

When software collapses into one interface, attention revenue is what’s at risk

“I think that all of software is going to collapse down into a single, very, very easy to use interface. Don’t get that confused with capability being limited.”

“if your business benefits from more transaction volume, even at the cost of less time on the application, then you’re going to be in a really great spot”

The PM Translation

Run his test on AlphaSense: how much of the value is analysts’ time in the search UI, and how much is the content itself? Agents that pull the content directly cut time in the app but should increase queries. The exposed part is seat pricing tied to time in the app.

The Investor Read

Noah splits revenue into two kinds: earned from user attention (ads, upsells, time in the app) or earned from delivering the underlying service. Attention-heavy businesses get squeezed; service businesses gain as friction drops to near zero. It’s a quick screen for every portfolio company — though it’s his argument, not data (he admits he doesn’t have Uber’s or DoorDash’s numbers).

In Practice

Place each angel holding on the attention-vs-service scale, and flag anything that earns more than 50% of revenue from attention.

04On Trust

Trust is the activation metric

“time to first credit card or time to first account password or time to first sensitive piece of information, these are proxies for trust.”

“when users connect at least one piece of sensitive information to instinct and really trust instinct, there’s an 80% retention rate”

The PM Translation

The DDW equivalent is time to the first confidential upload — a CIM or a data room document. The 43% drop-off between the doc picker and workspace creation you reported in July is probably this trust step, not a UX problem.

The Investor Read

About 40% of Instinct users share a credit card within three weeks. Ask founders for retention split by whether users shared sensitive data. Noah gives no time window for the 80% figure, so press on that.

In Practice

Pull DDW cohorts and compare retention for users who uploaded their own deal docs vs. users who only used AlphaSense content.

05On Safety

Keep the checker separate from the agent

“for every action that instinct might take or for every thought that it might have, that is being actively monitored by a system that is decoupled from instinct itself, which is able to pause it, intercept it, to approve or disapprove of what might happen next before it takes the action.”

“It is validated and scrutinized by something that is decoupled from the same incentive system as the underlying agent.”

The PM Translation

Once DDW agents take actions — sending to counterparties, writing to a CRM, touching material non-public information — the check on claims and citations should be a separate system. An instruction inside the agent’s prompt isn’t enough. This fits with Hebbia’s review layer and Rogo’s audit trail.

The Investor Read

Diligence question for any agent startup: is safety a separate system, or an instruction in the prompt? Instinct has two: “firewalls” that screen incoming content before the agent sees it, and an outside action monitor that can pause or block any step before it runs.

In Practice

Spec a pre-output check: every number in an IC memo gets matched against its cited source before the memo is shown.

06On Product Feel

Make it understandable before making it more capable

“let’s not focus on capability. Let’s only focus on understandability. So how much does a user understand about what’s happening? What is their ability to predict what will happen…”

“…maybe you front load some of the information so that the user really gets it in the first 30% and then can optionally read the rest.”

The PM Translation

DDW memos are long. Put the answer up front, and make it predictable what each agent will produce before the user runs it. The finding that users ask for custom agents but rarely build them may be an understandability problem.

The Investor Read

He tests hard-to-measure qualities like trust with staged rollouts: himself, then the team, then the early access group, then the public. Growth: 10–11% a day with $0 on marketing so far, invite-only with 5 invites per user, and invites selling on eBay for about $300 (his own account, not verified).

In Practice

Rewrite the opening of one GA agent’s output so the answer fits in two lines, then test it with 3 power users.

07On Direction

Standing goals instead of one-off tasks — and the compute bet behind them

“I think that’s the meta level picture is moving away from individual use cases over to higher level objectives.”

“doesn’t actually need to finish in hundreds of milliseconds. [It] needs to finish in minutes or even hours.”

The PM Translation

DDW agents mostly produce one-off deliverables today. The next step is standing assignments, like “watch these 5 targets and flag anything that breaks the thesis.” That work can run in the background as batch jobs, which Noah says runs 3–8x more efficiently on the same compute.

The Investor Read

Compute takes several months to bring online, and “if you’re wrong, you’re wrong by three or four x.” He spends about 40% of his time on this, and claims frontier-level quality “at a cost that is very low.” Ask founders what share of their workload is batch vs. live, and what each user costs per day. Patrick, not Noah, put the latest round at roughly $1B at a ~$10B valuation.

In Practice

List which DDW agent runs could tolerate minutes-to-hours latency, and ask engineering for the cost difference on batch inference.

08On Moats

An agent-to-agent network with access set per relationship

“It’s not like a friend graph where you’re just saying like I’m connected to you and that’s it. It’s I’m connected to you. I trust you. I have also configured it in this way. So it’s that there’s varying levels of access.”

The PM Translation

Diligence involves many parties: the deal team, co-investors, lenders, advisors. Scoped sharing between agents (“the lender’s agent sees the model tab and the Q&A log, nothing else”) would make DDW the place those parties coordinate — the layer Rogo said it’s building.

The Investor Read

This network is his moat against incumbents who can reach 1–2 billion users on day one. Each connection carries its own access level, and if someone digs beyond what they were granted, the agent tells its owner. Look for startups where the network gets stronger with each new relationship, not just each new user.

In Practice

Map one live deal’s parties and what each should be able to see. That map is the permissions spec.

The Single Question

When deal teams talk to a single agent, will AlphaSense be the storefront they talk to, or the merchant paying someone else’s take rate?

Before reading

This guy is a genius! He really understands the distribution game and what it means to own the data and the storefront to the experience itself. It gives you a ton of leverage against the big players because you’re becoming their distribution channel, and by providing benefits to the user, you’re able to monetize, not on the user base, but from the merchants in the form of take rate.

“…maybe you front load some of the information so that the user really gets it in the first 30% and then can optionally read the rest.”

This was really genius, and it shows how he has user empathy because he knows that humans are bombarded with information and we need to quickly get to what we need within a short amount of time. This is definitely an expression of user obsession.

Lesson 08 · Trust Network

“It’s not like a friend graph where you’re just saying like I’m connected to you and that’s it. It’s I’m connected to you. I trust you. I have also configured it in this way. So it’s that there’s varying levels of access.”

What an interesting take on the network effect. In the past, it was kind of the same way: when you go on Facebook, you want your friends to be on it. Now it’s like taking advantage of this viral spread of trust and getting more people onto Instinct, and letting the agents communicate with other people’s agents so that you can abstract away a lot of the mundane things that shouldn’t take up our time. It’s kind of like an assistant for everyone, without you having to look through your calendar to align on the next meetup time with your friend.
