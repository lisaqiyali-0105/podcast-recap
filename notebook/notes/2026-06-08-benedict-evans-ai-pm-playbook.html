Benedict Evans × PM
Overview

Podcast Recap · For Product Managers

6 lessons Benedict Evans
taught me about AI.

AI is as big a deal as the internet — and only as big a deal as the internet. We’re in 1997. Most of what gets built hasn’t been built yet. And the jobs you’re worried about disappearing? History says new ones come. They always have.

The most rational take on AI you’ll hear this year →
01
On Timing

We’re in 1997.
Stop acting like it’s 2010.

We’re in 1997. It’s very exciting. Most stuff kind of doesn’t work yet. Most of the stuff that people are going to do hasn’t been built yet. And it’s not really clear how any of it’s going to work when it does work.

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

The pressure to have a fully-formed AI strategy right now is mostly noise. We’re in the “what is this?” phase — not the “who won?” phase. Even Benedict’s 80-slide deck is essentially 80 slides of “we don’t know yet.” The companies that dominated mobile in 2010 weren’t obvious in 2000. The person who predicted Yahoo! would lose to a weird search company with a colorful logo was not the consensus view. That’s not an excuse for inaction — it’s an excuse to stop treating every AI hype cycle as if you’re already behind.

In Practice

Next time someone asks “what’s our AI strategy?”, reframe the question internally: what problems do our users have that weren’t solvable before? That’s the 1997 question worth answering. Not “which model should we use?”

02
On What Work Really Is

The task isn’t the job.
Know the difference.

Are you hiring them to get a 75 slide deck? Well, narrowly, Claude will make a really crappy version of that. But even if it was, that’s not what you pay them for. What you actually pay Bain to do is to go and walk all over your company and work out: yes, but why is it that you didn’t do that? And how do the politics of this work? And what do you actually need to do? The PowerPoint is just the task. But that’s not what you hired them for.

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

This is the core PM trap with AI features. We get excited about automating the visible task — the report, the summary, the draft — when the actual value users pay for is the judgment, context, and decision behind it. Benedict’s Amazon analogy makes it clean: Amazon gets you the SKU if you know what you want. But knowing what SKU to want? That’s a different job entirely. If your AI feature handles the PowerPoint but users still need someone to tell them what to put in it, you’ve saved two hours and left the real problem untouched.

In Practice

For any AI feature you’re building or evaluating, ask: is the task we’re automating actually the hard part — or is it the scaffolding? If it’s scaffolding, the real product opportunity is upstream.

03
On Jobs

You can’t see the new job
because it doesn’t exist yet.

Every time we have a new technology, it automates away a bunch of jobs and then that automation unlocks a bunch of new jobs. You can always see the job that’s going to go away. And you don’t know the new job because it doesn’t exist yet. And it’s like something that sounds dumb anyway, like “railway engineer” — what’s a railway? Why would that be a thing? Who would want to go that fast?

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

Your instinct that “people will adapt” is historically correct. What you noticed after listening — that evolution takes real time — is also correct, and Benedict validates it: enterprise adoption cycles are three to five years minimum. The jobs that disappear are, in retrospect, the crap ones. The new roles feel weird and pointless until they’re obvious. The FOMO isn’t just emotionally unproductive — it’s analytically wrong. Most enterprise users are not daily AI users yet. That gap between the tech-forward 20% and the mainstream 60% is the PM opportunity, not a threat.

In Practice

Stop benchmarking yourself against “AI-native” workers. Benchmark against your actual user. If your users are in that 60% still figuring it out, you’re not behind — you’re exactly on time.

04
On Competitive Moats

When everything’s a commodity,
distribution wins.

Google is using distribution to drive Gemini. What’s the difference between Gemini and Claude? If you’re using this stuff all day, then you know. But like, normal person, there’s no difference. Distribution of an adequate product — when the field is basically commodity — distribution and brand become a big deal.

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

In a world where foundation models are converging toward commodity pricing and comparable quality, the winner isn’t the best model — it’s the model that’s already there when the user needs it. The browser wars analogy is useful: Microsoft used distribution to dominate browsers, then discovered that winning browsers didn’t actually get them anything, because the value was further up the stack. Your AI feature doesn’t need to be the best. It needs to be in the workflow. Default status beats superior capability every time.

In Practice

Audit where your product isn’t in the user’s default workflow. An AI feature buried in a settings menu is not distribution. One that shows up in the flow the user already lives in — that’s the play.

05
On Disruption

You can’t predict
what gets disrupted.

You can’t look at a senior partner at a law firm and say, well, 17% of their work could be automated. This is horseshit. You can’t describe a profession like that. And the other side — you can’t predict which things are going to be exposed. Like personal trainers: I take my iPhone, balance it on the metal piece, and ask an AI to build me a training routine and watch me and tell me if I’m doing it right.

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

The frameworks for “AI exposure scores” by job type are false precision. As a PM, you’re also bad at predicting which features will get disrupted — not because you’re wrong about technology, but because you’re modeling the wrong variable. The disruption rarely comes from doing the same task cheaper. It comes from reframing what the task is. In 1997, the internet seemed irrelevant to taxi drivers. By 2010, it had restructured the entire industry — not by digitizing taxis, but by rethinking what “getting a ride” even meant.

In Practice

For your product category, ask: what’s the personal trainer equivalent? What does your product do that seems “human” — but could be reframed as a workflow an AI handles asynchronously? That’s where the blind spot is.

06
On What To Do About It

Dive in.
FOMO won’t save you.
Fluency will.

Don’t stick your head in the sand and say, I hate all of this stuff. That gives you a great feeling of moral superiority and you can go on Blue Sky and shout at everybody about how evil AI is. Great, I’m happy for you. But that’s not going to help. What helps is you diving into this completely — submerging yourself in it and coming out understanding what you can do with it, how this changes things.

Benedict Evans — Lenny’s Podcast

The PM Translation

This connects directly to your instinct after listening: the antidote to FOMO isn’t avoidance — it’s fluency. Benedict isn’t saying “learn to code AI.” He’s saying build a genuine understanding of what it can and can’t do in your specific context, so you can make good calls instead of reactive ones. That’s a PM superpower. The people who get it wrong aren’t the ones who moved too slowly — they’re the ones who moved fast without understanding the terrain. Discover as you use. Use as you discover. That loop is the whole strategy.

In Practice

Pick one AI tool adjacent to something you already do (synthesis, research, writing) and use it for 30 days on real work — not toy examples. The goal isn’t expertise. It’s developing the intuition for where it breaks down, so you stop being surprised by it in product decisions.

The Single Question

Is your AI feature automating
the task — or solving the job?

“What Amazon does is get you the SKU if you know what the SKU is. Claude can write you the code, but what code do you want? It can make you the features. Sure, but what features do you want? Who’s your customer? What’s the right product for that customer? How are you going to take it to market?”

— Benedict Evans, Lenny’s Podcast
