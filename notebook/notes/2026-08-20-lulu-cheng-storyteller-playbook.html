01 On Ownership

Narrative Sovereignty

"We should be in control of our narrative destinies and the stories that help us achieve the destiny. This is narrative sovereignty. You never just want to be dependent on someone else."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

Your MBA clients are about to enter a world where their story will be told by recruiters, alumni networks, LinkedIn algorithms, and hiring managers — all filtering through their own prism. If your clients don't own and actively shape their narrative, someone else will do it for them, poorly. As a coach who tells her own story publicly, this also means: your story on social media isn't marketing — it's your manifesto. It's how you demonstrate the very thing you teach.

Try This

For each MBA client, ask: "If you went silent for six months, what story would others tell about you based on the signals you're already putting out?" Use that gap between their intended story and their default story as the starting brief for your work together.

02 On Originality

The Anti-Template

"The alpha comes from the little cracks in reality that only you can see and that you can exploit. If you are just plastering over that with a templated version of something else, you are sacrificing the opportunity to be better."

"Ralph Waldo Emerson calls it a form of suicide because in imitating others, you are killing your own ideas."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

Every MBA candidate you coach will be tempted to use the "tell me about yourself" framework they learned in a career center workshop — the STAR method, the passion-pivot-proof LinkedIn structure. But those templates are the enemy of distinctiveness, and in a cohort of 200+ people applying to the same roles, distinctiveness is the only currency that matters. Your coaching creates alpha by treating every client as a unique case study rather than a personality type to be mapped onto an archetype.

Try This

In your next intake session, explicitly ban the three most common MBA narrative templates ("I've always been passionate about X," "I made an impact at Y by doing Z," "Now I want to leverage my background in..."). Force your client to describe their story in their own unscripted words, then find the crack in that raw material that no template would have surfaced.

03 On Audience

The Hero Is the Listener

"At the center of his story, the protagonist wasn't the human and it wasn't the person he was speaking to. He wasn't saying, 'You can go change the world.' Instead, he said the technology is coming whether you like it or not — you are potentially in its way."

"The Jetsons are the hero of the story. It is not called 'show about robots.' The human story is the hero and the technology is ambient."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

This is the single most practical storytelling principle for your MBA clients' social media presence: you are not the protagonist of your own LinkedIn post. Your reader is. A post about "what I learned during my summer internship" that never acknowledges what the reader can take from it is the Eric Schmidt commencement address — technically correct, emotionally empty. When your clients make their reader the hero (this is what's possible for you, this is what this pattern means for your career), the content stops feeling like self-promotion and starts feeling like a gift.

Try This

Run a "hero audit" on your client's last three LinkedIn posts. For each one, ask: who is the protagonist — you or the reader? Rewrite one sentence in each post to reframe the takeaway as something the reader walks away with, not something the client demonstrates.

04 On Conviction

The Manifesto and Intensity

"A good manifesto looks at the world and says, the people we're fighting have more numbers, money, force, and legitimacy than us. But we are going to beat all of that with more intensity. Intensity can overcome everything."

"The person in the middle has to be the most convicted, the most passionate, and have the clearest idea of the promised land."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

Your clients are entering recruiting as underdogs — no track record, no brand, often no network at the school they're targeting. The only asymmetric advantage available to them is intensity of conviction. A candidate who has clearly wrestled with what they believe, what they're fighting for, and why they are the one person who should be doing it will outperform a polished but generic candidate every single time. Your job as a coach is to help them find and name that intensity — not manufacture enthusiasm, but excavate the real thing.

Try This

Ask each client: "What is the thing you would do even if nobody paid you, nobody promoted you, and nobody praised you for it?" That answer is their manifesto. Write it down. Test every piece of their narrative against it — does this detail serve the manifesto or dilute it?

05 On Distribution

Go Direct

"If you're doing something unconventional, far outside the mainstream, you do not want this to be filtered through somebody else's prism. You lose the intensity along the way because it becomes watered down."

"You and I both care about a shockingly small number of people's opinions. And these are people that are like a text message away."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

For MBA candidates, "going direct" means identifying the 5–10 specific people whose opinion of them actually determines their outcome — a partner at the firm, an alum in the group, a hiring manager — and communicating directly with them in the places those people actually pay attention, rather than optimizing for the general LinkedIn feed. For you as a coach building your own public presence: you don't need a huge following. You need the right 50 people to see your work and feel like it's speaking to them.

Try This

Help each client build a "decision-maker map" — the actual 5–10 people who will determine their outcomes this recruiting cycle. For each person: where do they pay attention (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, podcasts, specific alumni events), and how does your client get in front of them directly rather than through a generalized application funnel?

06 On Authenticity

Real vs. Fake (The Beauty Test)

"When that happens, I write off the entire company as fake. I don't think this was fake engagement. I think to myself, it's a fake company with a fake founder."

"Fake things are not beautiful... Beauty is a stamp of authenticity."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

LinkedIn is full of MBA candidate content assembled from a checklist — the "excited to announce" opener, the three-bullet lesson format, the humble-brag closer. Sophisticated readers — the exact recruiters and operators your clients want to reach — can spot it instantly. Lulu's insight is that real things are beautiful because someone put love into them; fake things are orphans. Your coaching's differentiator is helping clients produce content that is undeniably theirs — with a specific point of view, a real opinion, an original observation — because that's the only content that passes the beauty test.

Try This

Introduce "the stranger test" in your coaching: would a complete stranger, reading this post with no context about who wrote it, be able to tell it came from this specific person and no one else? If the answer is no, it needs another pass.

07 On Control

Fill the Narrative Vacuum

"We all have a form of referential mania. We need to believe that things around us are pointing to something. And if we're not told what that is, we will try to fill it in ourselves because our brains crave a story."

"It's the space in between the facts that ties them into a story — that is our responsibility to fill in. Because if you let people fill it in haphazardly, they might end up with any conclusion that you don't control."

The Founder & Storyteller Lens

Every MBA candidate has a series of facts — school, company, role, project — that recruiters and interviewers will read before they ever meet them. Those facts create a narrative vacuum, and every reader will fill it in. The question is whether your client fills it in first, deliberately, or lets the reader make assumptions. A gap year, a nonlinear career path, a pivot from one industry to another — these all look like red flags if left unfilled, and like intentional moves if your client gets there first with a coherent throughline.

Try This

Map your client's resume as a "facts list" — strip out all interpretation and just list the raw data points. Then ask: "If a skeptical reader saw only these facts, what story would they construct? Is that the story you want told?" The gap between those two answers is exactly the work you do together.

The Single Question

If the hero of your story must always be the person listening — then who are you actually writing for, and does everything you put out reflect that?
